The amount in orbit is astonishing

There are 30,913 amount of debris actively orbiting the Earth, and those are only the trackable ones. That's 138 pieces of debris for every 1 space launch. And remember, these are only pieces that are large enough to track! There are potentially millions of pieces of debris but because they are smaller than 5cm we cannot track them.

Debris hurdles through space at lethal speed

Space debris can travel up to speeds of 18,000 mile per hours which even something the size of a nickel can cause billions of dollars of damage and cascading effects across our planet’s orbit.

And it is an existential threat to our species’ future

Donald Kessler coined a term called the Kessler effect in which the amount of debris encircling our planet becomes too numerous, entombing our species to the Earth’s surface and rendering any efforts to venture further out into space too dangerous.

Major Space Debris Causing Incidents

Space debris didn't get this bad gradually. Major incidents scattered debris by the thousands in just mere moments. Just the top 4 incidents make up 27% of the total amount of current debris orbiting the planet. The Fengyun 1C incident and Cosmos 1408 incidents were caused by their countries shooting them down with special missiles for military exercises. Cosmos 2251 collided with another satellite and most recently a malfunction with Intelsat 33e caused it to get too drawn into the Earth's Orbit causing it to break up.

Making Landfall

Debris reenters Earth's atmosphere everyday, typically burning up on descent. However several times a year very large pieces of space junk make landfall and have injured people and damaged property.

Looking Ahead

As we accelerate the pursuit of space exploration, we must also recognize that with more space exploitation and exploration, more growing consequences of our actions beyond Earth. Launches are becoming more frequent, but so is the accumulation of debris: a yet invisible affect that threatens the very progress we celebrate.

The question now is, can we continue to look outward without compromising our future?
